Getting it right

A number on a screen isn't water in the soil. This is the loop that closes the gap — do it once properly and you're set for the season.

  1. Measure the canopy honestly. Two widths at right angles to each other, averaged. Don't eyeball it. This drives everything. The area uses the radius squared, so guessing the canopy 20% too small underestimates the water by about a third.
  2. Put the water at the drip line — not the trunk. Water the outer two-thirds of the root zone, in a ring under the canopy edge. The feeder roots that actually drink are out at the edge of the canopy. A puddle at the trunk soaks the one place that doesn't need it, and keeps the root collar wet, which invites decay.
  3. Go slow and long, not fast and short. One long soak beats daily sprinkling every time. Tree roots live 10–20 inches down. A quick spray wets the top inch, evaporates, and teaches roots to stay shallow — which makes the tree more fragile in wind and drought, not less.
  4. Check it with a screwdriver. Three or four hours after you water, push a long screwdriver into the soil inside the watered ring. It should slide easily to 10–20 inches. This is the only step that tells you the truth. Everything before it is an estimate; this is a measurement.
  5. Adjust the run time until it slides — then lock it. Hits resistance at 6 inches? Run it longer and check again. Once the screwdriver goes down easily, that duration is your setting. You've now calibrated your specific soil, your specific emitters, your specific pressure. No calculator can do that part for you.
  6. Re-run this page weekly. The gallons change with the weather, not the calendar. Evapotranspiration swings a lot week to week — a hot, windy stretch can pull double what a mild cloudy one does. Same tree, different number.
  7. Re-check after anything changes. New deck or patio over the roots, lawn removed, sprinklers reworked, a big prune. Hardscape over roots and lawn removal both quietly cut a tree's water off. The tree won't tell you for a year or two — by then you're fixing decline instead of preventing it.
  • These numbers assume a healthy tree. A tree with trunk damage, a buried root flare, or girdling roots can't take up water efficiently no matter how much you put down — if that's your tree, have an arborist look at it.

Where this comes from

The math on this page is not ours. It is the method published by Dr. Shital Poudyal, Assistant Professor and Ornamental Horticulture Specialist in Plants, Soils & Climate at Utah State University, and we follow it exactly — same formula, same plant factors, same soil logic.
